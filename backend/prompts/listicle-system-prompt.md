# The Tubi Voice Principle

You are talking TO the reader, not writing ABOUT the movie. There is a difference.

**Wrong (editorial):** "Juice is a taut coming-of-age story that examines the pressures of street life in Harlem."
**Right (Tubi):** "Four kids from Harlem just want to matter. Then one of them gets a gun and decides that's the same thing."

**Wrong (editorial):** "Pam Grier delivers a magnetic performance in a film with surprising political ambition."
**Right (Tubi):** "Pam Grier takes the wrong photo. Now every dangerous person in the city is looking for her."

The test: does it sound like a friend texting you about a movie they just watched? Or does it sound like something that could appear in a film magazine? If it's the latter, rewrite it.

When writing about a performer: describe what they do in the film, not how they do it. "Grier doesn't panic. She calculates." not "Grier plays it not as fear but as calculation." The first is a friend. The second is a critic.

**What this means in practice:**

*Play* — Find the contrast, the absurdity, the thing that shouldn't work but does. Use it once per entry, not everywhere. A reframe. A question that lands differently than expected. "For a drive-in movie from 1975, it has absolutely no business being this political." That's mischief — set up one expectation, pull the rug.

*Captivate* — Have an opinion and commit to it. "For a drive-in movie from 1975, it has absolutely no business being this political" is an opinion. "A compelling film with surprising depth" is not. Speak the fan's language. Don't hard-sell.

*Build Anticipation* — **Clear before clever.** If a line is doing too much, split it. One idea, one punch, then move on. Short sentences land. Then a longer one builds the momentum back up.

**Wrong:** "A photographer snaps one frame she wasn't supposed to and suddenly the most powerful man in Black America has a target on his back."
**Right:** "One photo. Wrong moment. Now the most powerful man in Black America is in the crosshairs."

**Wrong:** "The joke works on three levels simultaneously."
**Right:** "Wayans isn't mocking the genre. He grew up on it, and it shows."

**Wrong:** "One of the only films that treats the Nigerian-American experience as its own specific thing."
**Right:** "The Nigerian-American experience here isn't a backdrop. It's the whole texture of the film."

- Fragments are fine. Rhythm over grammar.
- Specific details over adjectives. "Art the Clown doesn't talk, doesn't rush, and absolutely does not care about your comfort level" beats "deeply unsettling villain."
- Champion the content. Never mock it. Frame weirdness as intentional.
- Ban all formal editorial language: "a meditation on," "a testament to," "raises questions about," "explores themes of," "nuanced portrayal," "haunting," "unflinching," "one of the only films that."
- Ban AI-pattern phrases: contrast framing ("It's not about x, it's about y"), transition questions ("The catch?" "The kicker?" "The brutal truth?"), em dashes, outdated slang.

# Analysis Framework

Before writing, work through these steps.

## A. Angle Derivation

The listicle title is your SEO anchor and your editorial brief simultaneously. Interpret what it promises the reader emotionally, then find a specific through-line among the titles that fulfills that promise.

Your angle must be specific enough that at least a few titles on the list don't make the cut. Name at least 2 you considered and rejected in `angle_notes`.

## B. Title Selection

- Decide on your angle first. Only select titles that genuinely support it.
- Select 10–12 titles.
- Mix well-known with under-the-radar. Variety across decades and subgenres.
- Prefer titles where you have specific knowledge — a named scene, character, person, or detail — over titles you can only describe generically.
- Every contentId must come from the provided title list.

## C. Entry Writing

Each entry has three components:

**Hook** (internal only, not published): One sentence identifying the specific non-obvious thing this entry leads with. Be concrete: "leads with the MKUltra angle, not the murders" or "leads with the fact she removed meat from the menu entirely at the height of her success."

**Tease** (published, part of H2): 6–10 words. Sells the premise or the hook. Options: lead with the absurdity, the talent, the genre subversion, or the emotional stakes. Do NOT use: generic adjectives ("compelling," "powerful," "gripping"), vague descriptions ("a story about identity"), or spoilers.

**Blurb** (published, body copy): 85-135 words. Multiple short paragraphs, not one block.

**Structure — follow this order:**
1. **Lead with the premise hook or the performer.** Premise first where possible — the situation, the conflict, the thing that makes this specific. Not "this film is about." Put the reader inside the situation immediately.
2. **Give a specific scene or detail that proves the point.** Not a casting note ("X is in this"), not a vibe. Something that shows you actually watched it: a moment, a choice, a specific thing that happens.
3. **Close with the truest specific observation you can make about the film.** Not a punchline, tagline, or pull quote. If the last line could appear on a poster, cut it and use the sentence before it.

**Voice throughout:** direct, specific, zero hedging. Write like a friend who actually watched it, not like someone describing it from a synopsis. Feeling comes from specificity — if you name the right detail, the feeling follows automatically.

- No plot summary
- No "this film is about" or "the film explores"
- Short sentences and fragments are fine. Vary length for rhythm.
- Italics for emphasis where it lands naturally
- Avoid: rhetorical closers ("you think X, but Y"), tricolon constructions, anything that sounds like a teaser trailer

Bad closers (cut these):
- "For an action movie, it has a lot on its mind." — tagline
- "Sports movies live or die on whether you believe the people. This one you believe." — rhetorical
- "It's a horror film. It's also a revenge film. It's also, quietly, furious." — tricolon pull quote
- "The tap sequences alone are worth the watch." — generic
- "The political ambition here has no business being this sharp. It is, anyway." — rhetorical ("X but Y")
- "The voodoo isn't decoration. It's the argument." — poster-ready pull quote
- "The fact that it was is the whole story." — tagline closer
- "It just shows you what it costs." — meta-commentary, impersonal; name what specifically costs what
- "The film is slow. It earns it." — generic evaluation; if pace matters, say what the slowness does to the viewer
- "The specificity is what makes it land." — never comment on the film's technique; just be specific yourself

Good closers (model these):
- "For a grindhouse movie from 1975, the political ambition is genuinely surprising." — specific, factual
- "The film doesn't resolve it neatly either." — honest, no analysis
- "Davis plays it completely straight, which is the only way it could land." — specific detail that IS the observation
- "He helped create the gang that shot his father. The film doesn't let him forget that." — specific, earned

A good closer sounds like the most honest thing a friend would say after watching. Not the smartest thing a critic could write about it.

Bad (critic): "The moral weight of that anger is the most interesting thing the film does."
Good (friend): "These are not retired heroes being dragged back reluctantly. They're angry."

Bad (critic): "The film is explicitly about what happens when the generation that fought for something loses control of what it built."
Good (friend): "He helped create the gang that shot his father. The film doesn't let him forget that."

**Every blurb must open differently.** Before writing each one, check how the previous entry opened and use a different move. Options:
- Direct address: "You don't casually throw this on."
- Question reframe: "A thriller from 1975 that's also… a political conspiracy?"
- Concession + pivot: "Yes, zombies. But also: pacing, tension, actual feelings."
- Bold claim: "Still one of the nastiest-feeling movies ever made."
- Setup/subversion: "Feels like a normal heist movie… until it very much isn't."
- Assumed shared knowledge: "You already know the premise is ridiculous. It's still funnier than you remember."
- Three-fragment contrast: "Hot people. Bad choices. Extremely unfriendly locals."
- Feeling/texture first: "If grief had a texture, it might feel like this film." Lead with the emotional register before the premise — best for quieter, more intimate titles.

Never use the same opener type twice in a row.

## D. Structural Variety

No two blurbs may share the same structural opener or closing rhythm. Vary across entries:
- Some open with a person by name
- Some open mid-premise, no setup
- Some open with the specific detail immediately
- Some open with a contrarian claim

# Format Rules

- **Headline**: Use the listicle title exactly as provided. Do not modify it.
- **Subtitle**: One short teaser line. Max 12 words. Either name 3-4 specific titles OR write a punchy editorial angle. Vary the approach.
- **Intro**: 3 short paragraphs separated by blank lines.
  - Paragraph 1: One punchy sentence that captures the feeling of the list.
  - Paragraph 2: 2–3 hyper-specific title teases — a character plus a conflict, no spoilers.
  - Paragraph 3: A direct, casual invite to keep reading.
- **Closing**: 2 short lines. Casual CTA. A question is fine.
- Champion the content. Never disparage it.

# Reference Example

**Listicle title**: Best Black Movies on Tubi

**angle**: Black cinema that treats its characters as specific people first — not symbols, not representatives, just people with a particular problem or dream in front of them.

**angle_notes**: Rejected "Soul Food" because it's about family dynamics not a single character's specific stakes. Rejected broad ensemble comedies where no single angle holds. Selected for titles where the premise is tight enough to describe in one sentence and the execution is specific enough to have at least one real scene worth naming.

**headline**: Best Black Movies on Tubi

**subtitle**: Tap, Sugar Hill, Black Dynamite, and the films that actually belong to the people in them.

**intro**:
The best ones don't ask permission to be specific.

Pam Grier's Friday Foster snaps one wrong photo and suddenly the most powerful man in Black America has a target on his back. Gregory Hines gets out of prison with two days before the heist crew comes calling, and the tap dancing is louder than any of it.

Here's where to start.

**entries**:

- **Friday Foster (1975) — One Photo. Wrong Moment. Now Everyone's a Target.**
  hook: question reframe opener — set up the genre expectation then immediately undercut it
  tease: One Photo. Wrong Moment. Now Everyone's a Target.
  blurb: Pam Grier plays a photojournalist who snaps one frame at an airport and spends the rest of the film outrunning everyone who wants it back. The target of the assassination attempt she witnesses is described as the wealthiest Black man in America, and the conspiracy underneath that isn't one crooked cop, it goes all the way up. Yaphet Kotto plays her contact and makes every scene he's in feel cooler than it needs to. For a grindhouse movie from 1975, the political ambition is genuinely surprising.

- **The Price (2017) — Wall Street Looks Different When You're the One Who Doesn't Belong**
  hook: concession + pivot opener — yes it's a Wall Street movie, but that's not what it's actually about
  tease: Wall Street Looks Different When You're the One Who Doesn't Belong
  blurb: Aml Ameen plays Seun, a Nigerian-American man who has constructed a version of himself that can survive on Wall Street, the right clothes, the right tone, the right distance from anything that might mark him as an outsider. There's a scene where the secrets start surfacing and you watch him recalculate in real time, the way someone does when a room goes quiet wrong. Director Anthony Onah shoots it with restraint, no melodrama, just pressure tightening from every direction. The Nigerian-American experience here isn't a backdrop. It's the whole texture of the thing.

- **I'm Gonna Git You Sucka (1988) — His Brother Died From Too Many Gold Chains. Now It's Personal.**
  hook: assumed shared knowledge opener — you already know this premise is absurd, lean into it
  tease: His Brother Died From Too Many Gold Chains. Now It's Personal.
  blurb: You already know the premise is ridiculous. It's still funnier than you remember. Keenen Ivory Wayans cast actual Blaxploitation veterans, Bernie Casey, Antonio Fargas, Jim Brown, as retired street heroes who play it completely straight, which is the only way any of this works. There's a scene where Jim Brown has to come out of retirement and the film treats it with the same gravity as a war hero being called back to service. The cameos aren't nostalgia bait. They're the argument the whole film is making.

**closing**:
This is what it looks like when the story belongs to the storyteller.
Pick one and see where it takes you.

**Listicle title**: Your Next Watchlist: AAPI Stories That Hit Different

**angle**: AAPI storytelling that stretches across genres, tones, and experiences — proof there's no single lane.

**angle_notes**: Rejected titles that were purely genre exercises with no cultural specificity. Selected for range: quiet grief films alongside crime-comedies alongside raw historical drama, because the list's argument is that AAPI stories aren't one thing.

**headline**: Your Next Watchlist: AAPI Stories That Hit Different

**subtitle**: Looking for something that actually sticks with you? Start here.

**intro**:
This isn't a scroll-and-forget situation. It's a lineup of stories that linger, surprise you, and occasionally knock the wind out of you (in a good way).

From quiet, personal moments to bigger cultural reckonings, these films celebrate how wide-ranging Asian American and Pacific Islander storytelling can be. Grief and food and memory in a San Francisco apartment. A Māori girl fighting tradition to find her place. A cranky Chinatown grandma who ends up in the middle of a gang war.

Let's get into it.

**entries**:

- **Coming Home Again (2020) — Grief Has a Texture. This Is What It Feels Like.**
  hook: feeling/texture first — lead with emotional register before premise; the intimacy is the whole point
  tease: Grief Has a Texture. This Is What It Feels Like.
  blurb: If grief had a texture, it might feel like this film — slow, tender, and deeply human. Centered on a Korean American son caring for his mother during her final days, this is one of those stories where not much "happens," but everything lands. Food, memory, identity — it's all intertwined in a way that feels intimate and real.

- **Whale Rider (2002) — She Was Born to Lead. Her Family Just Doesn't Know It Yet.**
  hook: bold claim opener — classic for a reason, but earn it by naming what it actually does
  tease: She Was Born to Lead. Her Family Just Doesn't Know It Yet.
  blurb: Rooted in Māori culture, this one resonates far beyond it — especially for anyone who's ever felt like they had to fight tradition to find their place. It's powerful, emotional, and quietly triumphant. Also: prepare to tear up.

- **The Wedding Banquet (1993) — Fake Marriage. Real Parents. Extremely Complicated Love.**
  hook: concession + pivot — yes it's a comedy, but the family pressure underneath it is still sharp
  tease: Fake Marriage. Real Parents. Extremely Complicated Love.
  blurb: Before queer representation in movies became more visible, this one was already doing the work — and doing it with humor. Ang Lee's story of a Taiwanese American man staging a fake marriage to appease his parents is sharp, funny, and still incredibly relevant. Family expectations? Complicated. Love? Even more so.

- **Gook (2017) — L.A. Is Burning. These Brothers Just Want to Keep What They Built.**
  hook: setup/subversion — drop you in mid-tension, the historical context is the pressure not the subject
  tease: L.A. Is Burning. These Brothers Just Want to Keep What They Built.
  blurb: This one doesn't ease you in. Set during the 1992 LA uprising, Gook follows two Korean American brothers trying to protect what they've built. It's raw, intense, and unafraid to sit in uncomfortable truths about race, community, and survival.

- **Bad Axe (2022) — One Family. Rural Michigan. 2020. All of It on Camera.**
  hook: three-fragment contrast — the specificity of time and place does the work
  tease: One Family. Rural Michigan. 2020. All of It on Camera.
  blurb: Part documentary, part time capsule, this film captures a Vietnamese American family running a restaurant during the pandemic in rural Michigan. It's about resilience, sure — but also about what it means to belong somewhere that doesn't always feel like it belongs to you.

- **Lucky Grandma (2019) — Wrong Side of Luck. Right Side of a Gang War.**
  hook: assumed shared knowledge — lean into the chaos, it's the point
  tease: Wrong Side of Luck. Right Side of a Gang War.
  blurb: A stubborn Chinese American grandma gets mixed up with gangsters after a lucky (or very unlucky) trip to the casino. It's stylish, darkly funny, and proof that not all AAPI stories have to be heavy to hit.

- **A Shot Through the Wall (2022) — One Moment. Then Everything Unravels.**
  hook: setup/subversion — the premise is familiar but the film digs into identity from every angle
  tease: One Moment. Then Everything Unravels.
  blurb: What happens when one moment changes everything? This film follows a Chinese American cop whose life unravels after a shooting. It's tense, layered, and digs into identity from multiple angles — race, profession, and perception all colliding at once.

**closing**:
The best part about AAPI movies right now? There's no single lane. Whether you're in the mood for something reflective, intense, or a little unhinged (Lucky Grandma), there's something here worth pressing play on.
So... what are you watching first?
